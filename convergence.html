The short version: in the one-parameter problem the stochastic truncation error is already converged at the lowest order tested, so the accuracy is limited by the spatial grid. In the two-parameter problem that is no longer true.

Where the error actually lives

The domain is \(t\in[0,20]\), \(x\in[0,300]\), with a standard Gaussian initial condition. Grids of \(N\in\{75,\dots,1500\}\) points and PCE orders \(d\in\{2,\dots,6\}\) were swept, with Monte Carlo over \(10^4\) samples (generated with SELANSI) as the reference.

Refining the mesh from \(N=75\) to \(N=300\) removes most of the \(L^2\) spatial error; beyond \(N\approx 1200\) further refinement buys very little. Raising the PCE order from \(d=2\) to \(d=6\), by contrast, barely moves the mean and variance at all. Both observations point the same way: for a single uniform uncertain parameter, an order-2 expansion is already spectrally converged, and the dominant error is the spatial discretisation.

Consequence for cost. If the stochastic error is converged at \(d=2\), then paying for \(d=6\) is paying for nothing. At \(N=1501\) that is the difference between 911 s and 8107 s — a factor of about 9 for no gain in the quantities of interest.

Full-PDF accuracy, not just moments

Mean and variance are a weak test when the distribution can change shape. The PCE surrogate was therefore compared against the reference at \(t=20\) on the full PDF, ranked by total variation distance (mass redistribution) and Hellinger distance (shape, via square-root densities).

Where the worst case sits, and why

In the two-parameter problem the best and median cases are in the bimodal regime, where the surrogate captures both modes well. The worst case is in the strictly unimodal regime, furthest from the ensemble mean behaviour.

That ordering is the interesting part. For low \(\gamma_x\) the PDF can be bimodal for particular \(k_m\); as \(\gamma_x\) grows the second mode disappears, and the location of that transition depends on \(k_m\). Near the transition the map from parameters to solution is close to non-smooth, and a global polynomial basis converges slowly there — a well-documented limitation of global PCE. This is what forces \(d=3\) in the two-parameter case where \(d=2\) sufficed for one, and it is the argument for multi-element or adaptive stochastic Galerkin schemes with local refinement, or sparse PCE constructions, as the next step.

Against Monte Carlo

The most expensive configuration tested (\(N=1501\), \(d=6\)) finishes in 8107 s, about 2.25 h. Building the \(10^4\)-sample Monte Carlo reference with SELANSI needs roughly \(10^4\) deterministic runs at about 2 s each — on the order of 20 000 s, about 5.6 h.

The wall-clock ratio is real but it is not the main argument. The main argument is what happens after the solve: with the coefficients in hand, statistics and new parameter realisations cost a polynomial evaluation. Any task that needs many repeated evaluations — optimisation, parameter inference, sensitivity analysis, or the closed-loop controller on the control page — pays the solve once and then queries for free. Monte Carlo pays again every time.

Caveat on the timings. These are wall-clock times from one machine and one implementation, not a tuned benchmark. Treat the scaling trends as the result and the absolute seconds as indicative.
