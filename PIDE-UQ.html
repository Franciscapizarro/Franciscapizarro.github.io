The model

A single gene expresses one protein type \(X\) through its mRNA. Because mRNA degrades much faster than protein (\(\gamma_m \gg \gamma_x\)), protein arrives in uncorrelated bursts of mean size \(b = k_x/\gamma_m\), exponentially distributed. Under that assumption the protein probability density \(p(t,x)\) obeys the PIDE of Friedman et al., extended to the multivariate case by Pájaro et al.:

$$ \frac{\partial p}{\partial t}(t,x) = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\big[\gamma_x\, x\, p(t,x)\big] + k_m \int_0^{x} \beta(x-y)\, c(y)\, p(t,y)\, \mathrm{d}y , $$

with burst kernel \(\beta(x-y) = b^{-1}e^{-(x-y)/b} - \delta(x-y)\). The first term is degradation; the integral is burst production. The feedback enters through \(c(x) = [1-\rho(x)] + \varepsilon\,\rho(x)\) with the Hill function \(\rho(x) = x^{H}/(x^{H}+K^{H})\): a negative \(H\) means \(X\) activates its own transcription, which is the cooperative positive-feedback case that produces bimodal distributions.

Extension to \(n\) interacting genes

The single-gene equation above is the \(n=1\) case of a multivariate PIDE. For a network of \(n\) genes, each expressing one protein, the state is the joint density \(p(t,\mathbf{x})\) over the whole protein vector \(\mathbf{x}=(x_1,\dots,x_n)\), and every species contributes one degradation term and one burst term:

$$ \frac{\partial p}{\partial t}(t,\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}\big[\gamma_x^i\, x_i\, p(t,\mathbf{x})\big] + \sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(k_m^i \int_0^{x_i} \beta_i(x_i-y_i)\, c_i(\mathbf{y}_i)\, p(t,\mathbf{y}_i)\dd y_i\right), $$

with \(p:\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}\setminus\{0\}\) the temporal evolution of the protein probability density function, and each species carrying its own burst kernel

$$ \beta_i(x_i-y_i) = \frac{1}{b_i}\exp\!\left[-\frac{x_i-y_i}{b_i}\right] - \delta(x_i-y_i) . $$

Every kinetic parameter is now indexed by species: degradation \(\gamma_x^i\), transcription \(k_m^i\), mean burst size \(b_i = k_x^i/\gamma_m^i\). Each burst integral runs over one coordinate only, the \(i\)-th, with the others held fixed — \(\mathbf{y}_i\) denotes the state vector with its \(i\)-th component replaced by the integration variable \(y_i\),

$$ \mathbf{y}_i = (x_1,\dots,x_{i-1},\,y_i,\,x_{i+1},\dots,x_n) . $$

Where the coupling enters. The degradation terms are separable and each integral acts on a single coordinate, so the sums themselves do not couple the species. The coupling is contained in \(c_i\), which depends on the other proteins' levels. For a two-gene toggle switch, \(c_1\) is a decreasing Hill function of \(x_2\) and \(c_2\) of \(x_1\). If every \(c_i\) depends only on \(x_i\), the system reduces to \(n\) independent copies of the one-dimensional problem.

The joint density is discretised on an \(N^n\) grid, and the Galerkin projection below multiplies that system by the number of retained PCE coefficients. The results reported on this site are the one-dimensional case; the control page gives the two-dimensional toggle switch formulation.

Where the uncertainty goes

The kinetic parameters are not known exactly — measurement error and cell-to-cell variability both contribute. They are modelled as random variables. Two cases are studied:

Galerkin projection

The solution is expanded in a truncated PCE of total order \(d\),

$$ p(t,x,\gamma_x) \approx \sum_{j=0}^{L-1} a_j(t,x)\,\Phi_j(\gamma_x), \qquad L = \frac{(n_\theta + d)!}{n_\theta!\,d!} , $$

substituted into the PIDE, multiplied by each basis polynomial \(\Phi_k\), and averaged over the parameter distribution. Orthonormality (\(\langle\Phi_j\Phi_k\rangle = \delta_{jk}\)) collapses the time-derivative and burst terms onto mode \(k\), while the degradation term keeps full coupling through the moments \(\langle\gamma_x\Phi_j\Phi_k\rangle\). In vector form, with \(\mathbf{a}^L = (a_0,\dots,a_{L-1})^\top\):

$$ \frac{\partial \mathbf{a}^L}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\big[x\,\mathbf{V}\,\mathbf{a}^L\big] + \boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}(\mathbf{a}^L), \qquad V_{kj} := \langle \gamma_x\, \Phi_k \Phi_j \rangle . $$

\(\mathbf{V}\) depends only on the distribution of \(\gamma_x\) and the basis, so it is precomputed once. The key structural point: because \(\mathcal{B}\) does not depend on \(\gamma_x\), the burst term introduces no mode coupling in the one-parameter case.

Two uncertain parameters

With \(\boldsymbol{\theta} = [\gamma_x, k_m]\) independent, the basis is a tensor product \(\Phi_\alpha(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \phi_{\alpha_1}(\gamma_x)\phi_{\alpha_2}(k_m)\), and the burst operator factors as \(\mathcal{B}(p,k_m) = k_m(\mathcal{K}p)\). Now the burst term does couple modes:

$$ \frac{\partial \mathbf{a}^L}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\big[x\,\mathbf{V}\,\mathbf{a}^L\big] + \mathbf{M}\,\mathcal{K}(\mathbf{a}^L), \qquad M_{k\alpha} := \langle k_m\,\Phi_k\Phi_\alpha \rangle . $$

That extra coupling is why the two-parameter case needs a higher expansion order to reach the same PDF accuracy.

Why intrusive PCE at all

Once the coefficients \(a_j(t,x)\) are available, the mean and variance come straight out of them, \(\mathbb{E}[p]\approx a_0\) and \(\mathrm{Var}[p]\approx\sum_{|i|\ge 1} a_i^2\langle\Phi_i^2\rangle\), with no further sampling. Because the coefficients are stored as time-resolved fields, the full PDF for any parameter realisation at any stored time is a polynomial evaluation. That is what makes the downstream control loop affordable: the controller reads \(\mathbb{E}[p]\) from the ensemble at every step.
